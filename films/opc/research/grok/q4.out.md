I'll run a sourced research pass on one-person companies in the AI era, sticking to primary papers and platform data and marking anything unverified as not found.A one-person company can produce cheaply in 2026. The evidence says that does not, by itself, make the typical one make money. Lower coordination and production costs expand supply and stretch the top of the revenue distribution. Repeat customers, a way to be found, and cash on hand decide who is left with profit. No verified one-person, billion-dollar company was found as of October 30, 2026.

Revenue and profit are kept apart below. “Not found” means a primary source for that claim was not opened.

## 1. Why firms exist, and what AI has been shown to change

Ronald Coase, “The Nature of the Firm,” *Economica* 4, no. 16 (November 1937): 386–405. The passage is reproduced by the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas, *Economic Insights* 8, no. 3 (2003):

> “The main reason why it is profitable to establish a firm would seem to be that there is a cost of using the price mechanism.”

He then names the costs of discovering prices and of “negotiating and concluding a separate contract for each exchange transaction.” The firm stops growing where internal organization costs as much as another market purchase. The Royal Swedish Academy’s 1991 prize press release states the same result: a firm appears when production, contract, and administrative costs are lower inside it than through purchases and sales on the market. Coase’s own Nobel lecture (December 9, 1991) says he had, by summer 1932, “realise[d] that there were costs of using the pricing mechanism.”

**2023–2026 arguments that AI shrinks the firm are mostly unpublished, and they disagree with each other.**

Peer-reviewed and on point, but about digital transactions rather than a measured AI effect: Frank Nagle, Robert Seamans, and Steven Tadelis, “Transaction cost economics in the digital economy: A research agenda,” *Strategic Organization* 23, no. 2 (2025): 351–365, first published online January 18, 2024. From their working text:

> “increased reliance on digital transactions in the economy can, as seen through the lens of TCE, lead to both bigger firms (more vertical integration, e.g. Amazon and Salesforce) and an increase in transactions that occur via the market.”

They add that as AI tools spread, “it is likely that more and more industries will be impacted.” That is a research agenda, not an estimate of optimal firm size.

Not peer-reviewed, and pointed in opposite directions:

- Marc Bara, “The Firm Under AI,” SSRN, written February 27, 2026: AI “eliminates the cost basis” for the firm as coordination infrastructure, with a long-run prediction of “firm dissolution into AI-coordinated project networks.”
- Michael Harré and Paul Ormerod, “The Coasean Reversal,” SSRN, March 31, 2026: AI also creates “powerful countervailing forces,” so the firm may internalize work that used to sit in markets and clusters.
- Philip Bond / Man Group, August 10, 2026, summarizing a 2026 working paper: AI makes contract enforcement cheaper, which favors outsourcing, and internal coordination cheaper, which favors scale. “Whether AI makes firms bigger or smaller depends first on what ‘bigger’ means.”
- DeepMind Institute, “The Economics of Intelligence,” October 29, 2026: competence raises output per worker; coordination decides how large the organization can get. If coordination crosses what they call a “supercooperative” threshold, “bigger becomes better at every scale.”

**Empirical firm-size result that can be tied to AI: not found.** No statistical-agency or peer-reviewed study opened here identifies generative AI as the cause of a smaller average firm. What is measured is a longer rise in businesses with no employees, and a widening revenue gap among a selected set of solo-founded startups.

U.S. Census Bureau, “Number of U.S. Nonemployers Grew Faster Than Employer Businesses,” July 30, 2025:

> “From 2012 to 2023, the total number of nonemployers grew an average 2.7% annually, while employers averaged only 1.1% year-over-year growth.”

Over that window the Census says employers’ share of all U.S. businesses fell from 24.6% to 21.6%. Nonemployer establishment growth was 4.9% in 2021 and 4.7% in 2022, then 2.1% in 2023. Census QuickFacts gives **31,985,874** nonemployer establishments in 2024. A November 20, 2025 Census release says that in 2023 there were **30.4 million** nonemployer businesses with **$1.8 trillion** in receipts, inside **36.4 million** employer and nonemployer businesses with **$50.0 trillion** in receipts. Those receipts are gross, “before deducting expenses.” Nonemployer receipts were about **6.4% of 2023 GDP** ($1.8 trillion), not 6.4% of the $50 trillion receipt total. The 2024 national receipt total was not extracted from a Census narrative page in this pass.

Stripe, May 28, 2026, on Stripe Atlas C corporations only:

> “Solo startup founders, defined here as people who launched a startup through Stripe Atlas without any cofounders, account for 63% of C corps formed so far in the second quarter of 2026—an all-time high.”

Same post, revenue not profit: among Atlas solo startups, “median initial six-month revenue in 2025 was down 23% year over year, while revenue at the top decile was up 19%.” The top-decile/median revenue ratio in the first six months went from about **34×** four years earlier to **61×** in 2025.

A filing-based revenue-per-employee figure for a true one-person AI company was **not found**. Press figures for Cursor, Midjourney, and similar firms conflict on headcount and use annualized revenue, not profit. They are not used as facts here. Anthropic’s claimed revenue per employee was **not found** in a filing opened for this pass.

## 2. Superstars: small talent gaps, large revenue gaps

Sherwin Rosen, “The Economics of Superstars,” *American Economic Review* 71, no. 5 (December 1981): 845–858. The Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis (September 2002) quotes the paper:

> “Hearing a succession of mediocre singers does not add up to a single outstanding performance.”

> “When the joint consumption technology and imperfect substitution features of preferences are combined, the possibility for talented persons to command both very large markets and very large incomes is apparent.”

The mechanism is joint consumption (one performance serves a huge audience) plus poor substitution (two lesser sellers do not replace one better one). Market size then multiplies small quality differences into skewed income. Rosen did not publish a top-1% share for apps or software.

**Empirical test.** Felix Koenig, “Technical Change and Superstar Effects: Evidence from the Rollout of Television,” *AER: Insights* 5, no. 2 (June 2023): 207–223:

> “The launch of a TV station skewed the entertainer income distribution to the right, with most of the skew happening in the very top tail. The fraction of entertainers with incomes that reach the top 1 percent of the US wage distribution doubled.”

The AEA research highlight (July 24, 2023) adds that entertainment employment “declined by roughly 13 percent, with middle-income jobs suffering the heaviest losses.” A local station raised the local share of entertainers in the national top 1% by about 4 percentage points, from a base of about 4 points.

**Platform revenue, top versus the rest.**

- RevenueCat, *State of Subscription Apps 2026*, data on “over 115,000 apps” and “more than $16 billion in revenue.” One year after launch, “the average monthly revenue … is around $72, but the top 10% pull in $2,500+,” a **36×** gap. The top quartile starts above **$429** a month. This is subscription revenue on RevenueCat, not profit, and not the whole App Store.
- Sensor Tower, *State of Mobile 2026*, as summarized by analysts who say they read the report: the top 1% of apps accounted for about **92%** of 2025 in-app-purchase revenue, on the order of **$154 billion** of about **$167 billion**. A games-specific writeup instead says about **92.5%** of game IAP and **79.8%** of game downloads sat with the top 1% of publishers. The original Sensor Tower PDF was **not opened**. Treat “about nine-tenths of mobile IAP sits in the top 1%” as a secondary reading, and do not treat 92.0 versus 92.5, or “apps” versus “publishers,” as settled.
- Apple’s own June 4, 2026 release is a different number. The App Store ecosystem facilitated “over $1.4 trillion in developer billings and sales in 2025,” and “for more than 90 percent” of that, developers paid Apple no commission. Most of that is physical goods and services ($1.1 trillion in secondary reports of the Analysis Group study). It is not a top-1% share. An official Apple top-1% developer share was **not found**.
- Steam revenue share of the top 1% of games: **not found** in a Valve statistic. SteamDB’s public release counter, read during this pass, showed on the order of **18,400** games released in 2024, **21,200** in 2025, and **23,600** in 2026 year-to-date. Other October 2026 writeups of the same database, taken earlier in the month, put 2026 year-to-date nearer **20,000–21,400**. The count revises as titles are added or delisted. The robust fact is the level: annual releases are now above 20,000, against under 10,000 in 2020. That is supply, not revenue.
- YouTube, Korean National Tax Service data as reported by JTBC on February 16, 2026, for people who filed as one-person media creators for tax year 2024: **34,806** filers, total reported receipts **2.4714 trillion won**. The top 1% (**348** people) reported **450.1 billion won**, about **18%** of those receipts, or roughly **1.29 billion won** each. The bottom half averaged about **24.6 million won**. In Korean tax usage, 수입금액 is gross receipts, not profit. The NTS table itself was **not opened**. A Goldman Sachs note often cited as “the top 3% of YouTubers get 90% of creator earnings” was **not opened**; that figure is not used.

## 3. What happens to the median seller when production gets cheaper

**Freelancers, earnings.** Xiang Hui, Oren Reshef, and Luofeng Zhou, “The Short-Term Effects of Generative Artificial Intelligence on Employment: Evidence from an Online Labor Market,” *Organization Science* 35, no. 6 (November 2024): 1977–1989. Abstract:

> “freelancers in highly affected occupations suffer from the introduction of generative AI, experiencing reductions in both employment and earnings. … we find suggestive evidence that top freelancers are disproportionately affected by AI.”

In the results text: after ChatGPT, freelancers in more-affected occupations had about a **2%** drop in monthly jobs and a **5.2%** drop in monthly earnings relative to less-affected occupations, and were about **1.2%** less likely to get any job that month. Past quality did not protect employment. These are platform earnings, not business profit.

**Freelancers, demand.** Ozge Demirci, Jonas Hannane, and Xinrong Zhu, “Who Is AI Replacing? The Impact of Generative AI on Online Freelancing Platforms,” *Management Science* 71, no. 10 (2025); earlier CESifo Working Paper 11276 (2024). Abstract:

> “a 21% decrease in the number of job posts for automation-prone jobs related to writing and coding, compared to jobs requiring manual-intensive skills, within eight months after the introduction of ChatGPT. … the remaining automation-prone jobs are of greater complexity and offer higher pay. … Image-generating AI technologies led to a 17% decrease in the number of job posts related to image creation.”

That is a fall in postings, plus more competition for the posts that remain. It is not a measure of freelancer profit.

A 2025 population study in *Applied Economics Letters* (synthetic difference-in-differences on wages and employment) found **no statistically significant** wage or employment change between more- and less-exposed occupations, and explicitly contrasts that null with the online-platform papers. Online markets are not the whole labor market.

**New apps.** RevenueCat, same 2026 report: the share of newly launched apps reaching **$1,000** MRR within two years fell from **19%** to **17%**, and the share reaching **$10,000** MRR fell from **5.3%** to **4.6%**. Median year-over-year MRR growth in the middle was about **5%**. The top 25% grew **80%**. The bottom 25% fell **33%**. The top 10% grew **306%**. Again, revenue.

**Solo startups on Stripe.** Same May 28, 2026 Atlas analysis: the median solo founder’s early revenue fell while the top decile’s rose, so the gap widened from 34× to 61×. AI-native solo startups “generated almost twice the revenue” of other solo startups by year two, and “revenue at the 99th percentile was nearly the same” for AI-native and other startups. The lift was from about the 50th to the 95th percentile, not from one giant outlier. By month 24, median solo B2B revenue was “more than four times” median solo B2C revenue. Top-decile multifounder startups still had **53%** more revenue than top-decile solo founders at month 24. Among bootstrapped firms at the 99th percentile, the gap was about **5%**.

**Steam.** More releases, documented above. Median revenue or profit per new Steam release: **not found**.

## 4. The one-person billion-dollar bet, and what has actually been verified

Sam Altman, as reported by *Fortune*, February 4, 2024, from a conversation with Alexis Ohanian:

> “In my little group chat with my tech CEO friends there’s this betting pool for the first year that there is a one-person billion-dollar company. Which would have been unimaginable without AI and now will happen.”

That is a bet on **valuation**, not on profit or revenue.

Dario Amodei, at Anthropic’s Code with Claude conference, as reported by *Inc.* (May 23, 2025) and ZDNET (May 22, 2025): asked when the first billion-dollar company with one human employee would appear, he said **2026**, and later put the chance at about **70 to 80 percent**. He added it would be “in an area where you don’t need a lot of human-institution-centric stuff to make money,” and named proprietary trading as his first guess. A late-October 2026 social clip paraphrases him as saying two-person AI companies have already reached $1 billion and one person is “close, several hundred million dollars.” The companies were not named in that paraphrase. Those names were **not found** in a filing or in Amodei’s own published text.

**Verified one-person, billion-dollar company as of October 30, 2026: not found.**

The best-documented near case is Base44, and it fails both parts of the claim.

Wix press release, June 18, 2025, via Nasdaq:

> “Wix acquired Base44 for initial consideration of approximately $80 million plus additional earn-out payments paid through 2029.”

Wix expected “approximately $25 million in retention bonus payments” to Base44 employees in 2025, inside that initial consideration. Globes the same day: the company “only has six employees.” TechCrunch, citing Wix: eight employees, and about $25 million of the headline figure was their retention pay. It was not a one-person firm at closing.

Wix’s 2025 annual report, Note 3, filed in 2026 (figures in thousands of dollars):

> “The total purchase consideration for the acquisition of Base44 was $92,158, consisting of cash payment of $18,058 and contingent consideration … with an acquisition-date fair value of $74,100.”

So the accounting price at the June 13, 2025 close was **$92.2 million**, of which **$18.1 million** was cash and **$74.1 million** was the booked value of earn-outs, not cash paid that day. Later cash earn-outs exist; a single audited “total cash paid to the founder through October 2026” figure was **not found** in the note opened here. Calling the deal “$80 million cash to one person” does not match the filing.

Revenue after the sale, from Wix’s own releases, still not profit and no longer a solo firm: about **$100 million ARR** by the March 4, 2026 year-end release (“just one year after its founding and 9 months after our acquisition”), and “~$150 million of ARR as of May” in the May 13, 2026 quarterly release. Founder posts, via TechCrunch, said May 2025 profit was about **$189,000**. That number is self-reported. Base44’s profit after it joined Wix was **not found**.

## 5. Distribution, repeat customers, cash, and the nonemployer majority

**Repeat customers inside the solo-startup sample.** Stripe, May 28, 2026:

> “Nearly 30% of customers at top-decile solo startups returned the following month, compared with 8% at middle-decile startups.”

By the start of year two, customers acquired in month one were “spending 47% more at top-decile startups than they were initially—about twice the increase seen at middle-decile startups.” Among solo B2B firms, top-decile founders “retained first-month customers at six times the rate of median founders.” Top-decile founders were also much more likely to bill on a recurring basis, and they sold into about 10 countries in month one versus about 3 for the median. International sales were **51%** of revenue at the top decile and **2%** at the median. All of this is revenue and retention, on Atlas companies incorporated in 2022–2023.

**Cash and survival.** JPMorgan Chase Institute, “Cash is King: Flows, Balances, and Buffer Days,” September 2016, from more than 470 million transactions by 597,000 small businesses, February–October 2015:

> “The median small business holds 27 cash buffer days in reserve.”

The 25th percentile held fewer than **13** days and the 75th more than **62**. Median daily inflow was **$381** and outflow **$374**. This is not a 2024–2026 update. A later Institute study of firms in six metro areas in 2019 found much shorter buffers for some owners (typical Black-owned firms **9–12** days, Hispanic-owned **11–14**, White-owned **17–21**). An Institute release of July 26, 2018: median small-business life expectancy across 12 sectors was **5.3 years**, and “nonemployer firms are five times more likely to exit the marketplace than they are to hire employees.”

**How many no-employee firms there are, and what they clear.** Census counts above: about four in five U.S. businesses have no paid employees, and their gross receipts are a few percent of all business receipts and about 6% of GDP. Gross receipts are not profit.

Profit comes from the IRS, and the population is different. IRS Statistics of Income, Table 1, nonfarm sole proprietorships, tax year 2023, released March 2026 (money amounts in thousands; read from the official spreadsheet):

| | All returns | Returns with net income |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Returns | 31,125,909 | 21,411,462 |
| Business receipts | $2.063 trillion | $1.691 trillion |
| Net income less deficit / net income | $377.2 billion | $530.8 billion |

Mean receipts are about **$66,300**. Mean profit, counting losses, is about **$12,100**. About **31.2%** of returns (9.7 million) reported no net income. Among those that did, mean profit is about **$24,800**. The same table shows **$175 billion** of payroll, so many Schedule C filers have employees. They are not Census nonemployers. For tax year 2022, the IRS *SOI Bulletin* (Spring 2025) says profits were **19.7%** of business receipts, “the lowest it has been since 1990,” on $2.081 trillion of receipts and $410.7 billion of profits.

A Gusto figure that 3.6% of solopreneurs earn over $1 million was **not found** in a Gusto primary release opened here. Stripe’s Emily Sands, in the Stripe Sessions 2026 keynote (YouTube transcript, April 30, 2026), said the number of U.S. solopreneurs “doing over 100,000 in revenue” was close to **5 million**, and that Atlas companies incorporated in 2026 were “tracking to five times the revenue” of the prior cohort at the same age. The transcript then says solopreneurism is “how close to 5 million Americans earn their living,” which is looser than the revenue sentence. It is revenue, from a spoken keynote, not an IRS profit table.

## What the evidence says decides the money

Production cost is no longer the scarce input in software, writing, images, and a large part of freelance knowledge work. Coordination costs are lower too, which is why more firms can be born with no employees. The binding constraints that still show up in the data are:

1. **Being found, and being bought again.** On Atlas, the top decile’s month-two return rate is about 30% against 8% in the middle, and half their revenue is international against 2% for the median.
2. **A market that does not treat sellers as substitutes.** Rosen’s condition, confirmed by Koenig for television and visible in mobile IAP and in RevenueCat’s 36× gap a year after launch.
3. **Enough cash to survive a stop in inflows.** The last large transaction study put the median small business at 27 days. Nonemployers, in Chase’s data, usually exit rather than hire.
4. **The gap between receipts and profit.** A mean sole proprietorship takes in about $66,000 and keeps about $12,000 after costs and losses. Nearly a third keep nothing.

AI raises the ceiling for a small number of sellers and, on the freelance and new-app evidence, does not lift the median. The one-person billion-dollar company remains a 2024–2025 prediction. The verified 2025–2026 case, Base44, is a small team that reached about $150 million of annualized revenue inside Wix, after a purchase-accounting value of $92 million.
